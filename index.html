The history of vaccines
Early History of Vaccination
1400s
The first form of the biotechnology of vaccination began in the 1400s. This process was known as variolation, and involved intentionally exposing healthy people to smallpox in order to prevent future illness.
1721
Lady Mary Wortley Montagu spread this process to Europe, inoculating her children with smallpox, a technique she observed in Türkiye. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1774
Benjamin Jesty hypothesized that cowpox, a bovine virus, could protect against the contraction of smallpox. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1796
Edward Jenner invented the world's first successful vaccine, inoculating a child with cowpox from a milkmaid. The child then made a full recovery. In July of the same year, Jenner inoculated the child with smallpox, and he did not get infected. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1872
Louis Pasteur created the first laboratory-made vaccine, a vaccine for cholera in chickens. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1885
Pasteur successfully prevented rabies using post-exposure vaccination. His patient received a course of 13 injections containing increasingly stronger doses of the rabies virus and survived. His treatment method was generally not successful, and his method of injecting humans with diseases directly had an uncertain success rate. (World Health Organization, 2021)
Europe, Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, 1721
Recent History of Vaccination
1918
The Spanish Flu killed millions of people globally, particularly affecting United States soldiers. This made the U.S. military prioritize the development of an influenza vaccine. Throughout 1918, the U.S. Army tested millions of doses, reaching no conclusions. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1937
Max Theiler, Eugen Haagen and Hugh Smith developed the 17D vaccine, protecting against Yellow Fever. It eventually reached over one million people, earning Theiler the Nobel Prize in 1951. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1939
Pearl Kendrick and Grace Eldering studied the efficacy of the whooping cough vaccine, reducing the number of infected children by 12.8 per 100, from 15.1 to 2.3 per 100. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1945
Thomas Francis Jr. and Jonas Salk researched the polio vaccine. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1955
The first effective polio vaccine developed by Jonas Salk was introduced. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1960
Czechoslovakia eliminated polio within its borders using Albert Sabin's oral polio vaccine. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1967
The WHO launched the Intensified Smallpox Eradication Programme, a program with the goal of eradicating smallpox in 30 countries. Despite the Cold War, both the USSR and the USA supported the WHO program. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1988
After having eradicated smallpox, the WHO launched a Global Initiative to eradicate polio. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1994
The Americas became the first WHO region to be certified free of wild poliovirus. (World Health Organization, 2021)
1995
Anne Szarewski led a team working on an HPV (Papillomavirus) vaccine. She later became the principal investigator for developing the bivalent HPV vaccine. (World Health Organization, 2021)
2002
Europe was certified free of wild poliovirus. (World Health Organization, 2021)
2006
The first HPV vaccine was approved. (World Health Organization, 2021)
Modern History
1999
A vaccine against rotavirus was approved, but its use increased intestinal problems. (World Health Organization, 2021)
2006
A new version of the rotavirus vaccine with a decreased risk of intestinal problems was developed. (World Health Organization, 2021)
2016
A successful vaccine was produced through the Meningitis Vaccine Project, cementing the significance of public-private development collaborations in creating vaccines. (World Health Organization, 2021)
2019
A pilot for the malaria vaccine was launched in Ghana and Kenya. RTS,S was the first vaccine to target the most dangerous and common strain of malaria in young children. (World Health Organization, 2021)
2020
The WHO declared the outbreak of Coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) a Public Health Emergency of International Concern. In March, the WHO stated that COVID-19 was a pandemic. Public-private collaboration was critical to the development of COVID-19 vaccines, including the first FDA-approved mRNA vaccine developed by Pfizer-BioNTech. (World Health Organization, 2021)
2021
The WHO prepared a global vaccine stockpile for Ebola. Vaccination inequalities became apparent, with 75% of the world's COVID-19 vaccine doses being administered in only 10 countries. The WHO prioritized vaccination for health workers and at-risk groups in low-income countries to lower fatalities and allow health workers to aid the sick. (World Health Organization, 2021)

History of Vaccination Methods
Direct Inoculation

The idea of inoculation was not invented by a single person. In communities around Africa and Asia, immunity was attempted through direct inoculation of diseased matter. This was often dangerous. In 1796, Edward Jenner heard about a disease, cowpox, that milkmaids would contract, which protected them from smallpox. Material from a sore on a person with cowpox was inoculated into a child, and this significantly reduced his reaction to a later exposure to smallpox (University of Oxford, 2026).

Vaccine comes from the Latin “vacca,” meaning “cow” (University of Oxford, 2026).

Attenuated Vaccines

In the 1800s, scientific experimentation on the subject of vaccines increased. After learning about Jenner's experiments on cowpox, Louis Pasteur learned that sources of disease could be weakened, or attenuated, so that they could trigger immunity without triggering disease (University of Oxford, 2026).

Pasteurisation: Pasteurization is the process by which milk, wine, and other food products are sterilized, allowing them to be consumed safely (Jambholkar, 2024).

Inactivated Vaccines

In the 1890s, researchers, including Robert Koch, learned that antigens from heat-treated bacteria could still make a properly functioning vaccine, despite no longer being alive. German and British scientists made new vaccines for diseases like the infamous typhoid fever, which were often used in military applications (University of Oxford, 2026).

Toxoid Vaccines

In the 1890s, Emil Behring learned that antibodies from inoculated horses could destroy the toxins produced by diphtheria, earning him the Nobel Prize. In the 1920s, Gaston Ramon learned that both toxins and pathogens could be deactivated, forming toxoids and safely inducing immunity. This method helped make vaccines for many fatal viral and bacterial diseases (University of Oxford, 2026).

Sub-unit Vaccines

In the 1960s and 1970s, the safety and efficacy of vaccines were increased using specific sub-cellular components of pathogens instead of the entire organism. This allowed the vaccine to aid the body in developing immunity without giving the whole pathogen to a patient. This allowed the development of vaccines for typhoid fever and hepatitis B. In the 1980s, scientists engineered harmless pathogen analogues that could produce these subunits without directly using the pathogens to form them (University of Oxford, 2026).

Nucleic Acid Vaccines

In the 2000s, the development of more sophisticated methods for engineering nucleic acids allowed scientists to create nucleic acid vaccines. Previously, to vaccinate someone, scientists would have to directly administer a piece of a pathogen or microbe. With nucleic acid vaccines, scientists can directly instruct cells to make antigens to develop immunity (University of Oxford, 2026).

Nucleic Acid Vaccines
The Most Recently Used Type of Vaccine

The primary type of vaccine used during the COVID-19 pandemic was not a traditional sub-unit or attenuated vaccine; it was a nucleic acid vaccine. This helped establish the importance of nucleic acid vaccine technology in preventing infectious disease (University of Oxford, 2026).

The Impacts of Vaccination

The impact that vaccination has on the quality of human life is significant. Vaccination can help fight against diseases by strengthening the body's ability to fight against pathogens and significantly reducing the likelihood of contracting diseases as a result (World Health Organization, 2021).

The Ethics of Vaccination

The ethical impacts were primarily concentrated in times when the risks of vaccination were significantly greater than they are currently. In the 1700s and 1800s, vaccination methods were generally significantly more risky than they are now (World Health Organization, 2021). The ethical concerns came from the risks involved in vaccination. If a doctor administered a vaccine, especially through direct inoculation, a patient could directly contract the very disease that was meant to be prevented (World Health Organization, 2021). In the modern day, the likelihood of contracting a disease from a vaccine is significantly lower, especially with innovations in bioengineering like nucleic acid vaccines (University of Oxford, 2026).

The Social Impact of Vaccination

The social concerns with vaccination are significant. There is no statistically significant evidence that vaccination increases mortality rates (McCarthy et al., 2016), but socially, there are many instances where misinformation regarding vaccination can cause people to avoid them (Brownstein, 2026). Many prominent figures in the American public, including current President of the United States, Donald Trump, have made statements against the usage of vaccines and Tylenol in 2025, claiming that their usage has a causal link to autism (Hamilton et al., 2025). These statements are false and have been disproven by decades of research. Many people trust their country's administration to make trustworthy, well-researched statements about public health information, and the spread of this misinformation has exacerbated the prevalence of social groups like the anti-vaccine movement (Brownstein, 2026; Hamilton et al., 2025).

The Health Effects of Vaccination

Lastly, in terms of health concerns and risks, the adverse effects of vaccines are not generally extreme, and when they are, they are rare; the benefits outweigh the risks. In terms of the mortality rate of vaccination, in a sample size of over 8 million vaccinations, not a single person aged 9 to 26 years old died as a direct result of vaccination, establishing no observed causal link between vaccination and increased mortality in that study (McCarthy et al., 2016). In terms of non-fatal adverse effects, in a sample size of 946 children aged 0 to 14 years old, only 2 children had to be treated by emergency services, and none had to be hospitalized (Carrasco-Garrido et al., 2004).

The Global and National Regulation of Vaccines
Canada's Approach

In Canada, health professionals, vaccine program decision-makers and general Canadian vaccine stakeholders often refer to the Canadian Immunization Guide (Government of Canada, 2026). The guide is in four parts: key immunization information, vaccine safety, vaccination of specific populations and immunization agents. Part 1 covers information regarding immunization in Canada, including the benefits of immunization, national guidelines for immunization and record keeping for vaccinations. Part 2 covers safety and vigilance, and procedures for complications like anaphylaxis and other acute reactions. Part 3 covers vaccination of specific populations, like infants born prematurely, people who are pregnant, workers and travellers. Part 4 covers specific vaccination methods and procedures like COVID-19, diphtheria toxoid and the measles vaccine. The Canadian Immunization Guide is updated regularly and is a significant source of information for health professionals in Canada on viral immunization (Government of Canada, 2026).

Canada's Approach

In Canada, not just anyone is allowed to administer vaccines. Only trained pharmacists, nurses, pharmacy staff and health care professionals are allowed to administer vaccines, subject to applicable provincial or territorial rules. Pharmacists and pharmacy staff are expected to have been trained in accordance with the Pharmacy Act of 1991 and the Regulated Health Professions Act of 1991 (Government of Ontario, 2025).

The Global Guidelines

In terms of global policy regulation, vaccination guidance is managed by the World Health Organization. It is the job of the WHO to give guidance to member states on evidence-based immunization strategies. These strategies are devised with advice from the Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE). Its role is to review evidence on immunization and develop recommendations for all age groups, which are outlined in WHO vaccine position papers.

Opinion Piece: Do I Agree with the Usage of Vaccines?
Author: Robert
Organization: INFO.Vaccine

I completely agree with the use of this biotechnology because the benefits of the usage of vaccines are significantly greater than the risks.

In an article published by the American Academy of Pediatrics, in a group of over 8,472,685 vaccinations, for age groups from 9 to 26 years old, there was no clear causal link between vaccination and an increased risk of mortality (McCarthy et al., 2016). Essentially, this study found no deaths causally attributed to vaccination in its sample of over 8.4 million vaccinations (McCarthy et al., 2016).

As for the risks of not vaccinating people, the clear risk is contracting the disease as a result of not being vaccinated. In New York in 2018, an importation of measles and a delay in vaccination caused an outbreak, affecting unvaccinated children especially. 649 cases of measles were reported, and 81.2% of the cases were in people 18 years old or younger. The cost of the measles outbreak was 8.4 million dollars (Zucker et al., 2020). Not vaccinating people, especially children, can directly lead to outbreaks, infecting many people and leading to hospitalizations (Zucker et al., 2020).

In conclusion, the study cited found no deaths causally attributed to vaccination in its sample, while the risks of not vaccinating patients include preventable infections and outbreaks.
